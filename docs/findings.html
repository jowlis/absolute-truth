 CMP-007: The question 'Do one-way functions exist (i.e., functions easy to compute but hard to invert on average)?' is classified as 'verifiable-in-principle' (type: verifiable). The existence of one-way functions is equivalent to P ≠ NP (Impagliazzo 1995 'five worlds': Algorithmica, Heuristica, Pessiland, Minicrypt, Cryptomania). If one-way functions exist, then secure cryptography (pseudorandom generators, digital signatures, private-key encryption) is possible. The question could be resolved by: (a) proving P ≠ NP and constructing explicit one-way functions, or (b) proving P = NP (which would imply no one-way functions exist). It is not verifiable-now and not purely-conceptual — it is a concrete mathematical question with major practical implications.

 CMP-008: The question 'Can the problem of logical omniscience in Bayesian epistemology be resolved — i.e., can a normative theory of rational belief simultaneously satisfy: (1) logical consistency (beliefs closed under logical consequence), (2) probabilistic coherence (Kolmogorov axioms), and (3) computational feasibility (bounded reasoning resources)?' is classified as 'purely-conceptual' (type: conceptual). The problem (Hintikka 1962; Garber 1983; Hacking 1967) is that standard Bayesian agents are logically omniscient (assign probability 1 to all tautologies, 0 to contradictions, and respect logical entailment), which is computationally impossible for bounded agents. Proposed solutions (Garber-style, Hacking-style, logical induction, computational Bayesianism) involve modifying the normative framework. The question is purely-conceptual because it asks which normative ideal we should adopt for bounded reasoners — there is no empirical fact of the matter about 'the correct normative theory' independent of the choice of normative criteria.

verifiable · 0.98
 The Mandelstam-Tamm (MT) quantum speed limit bounds the minimum evolution time \tau between an initial pure state |\psi(0)\rangle and an orthogonal (or distinguishably evolved) final state under a time-independent Hamiltonian H as \tau \geq \frac{\pi \hbar}{2 \Delta E}, where \Delta E = \sqrt{\langle H^2 \rangle - \langle H \rangle^2} is the energy standard deviation (dispersion) in the state. For mixed states, \Delta E is replaced by the Bures-angle rate or variance \Delta H.

 Domain boundaries and validity conditions for the Mandelstam-Tamm speed limit require: (1) A Hilbert space \mathcal{H} of arbitrary dimension (finite or infinite); (2) A time-independent Hermitian Hamiltonian operator H bounded from below with a well-defined finite energy dispersion \Delta E < \infty; (3) Pure or mixed states \rho(t) undergoing unitary evolution via the von Neumann / Schrödinger equation \partial_t \rho = -\frac{i}{\hbar}[H, \rho]. For systems with infinite energy dispersion (\Delta E \to \infty), the Mandelstam-Tamm bound becomes trivial (\tau \geq 0), necessitating complementary bounds such as Margolus-Levitin.

 The Margolus-Levitin (ML) theorem bounds the minimum evolution time \tau for a quantum system to evolve from an initial state \psi(0) to an orthogonal state \psi(\tau) \langle \psi(0)|\psi(\tau) \rangle = 0 under a time-independent Hamiltonian H with ground state energy set to zero (E_0 = 0), given by \tau \geq \frac{\pi \hbar}{2 \langle H \rangle}, where \langle H \rangle = \langle \psi(0)| H |\psi(0) \rangle$ is the mean excitation energy above the ground state.

verifiable · 0.97
 Domain boundaries and complementary utility of the Margolus-Levitin theorem require: (1) A quantum system governed by a time-independent Hamiltonian H bounded from below (with ground state energy convention E_0 = 0); (2) Finite mean excitation energy \langle H \rangle < \infty; (3) The bound is tightest when the energy dispersion \Delta E is large compared to \langle H \rangle (e.g., for multi-level superposition states with high-frequency components), whereas the Mandelstam-Tamm bound is tightest when \Delta E is small compared to \langle H \rangle (e.g., for narrow two-level systems). Combining both yields the unified quantum speed limit \tau \geq \max \left( \frac{\pi \hbar}{2 \Delta E}, \frac{\pi \hbar}{2 \langle H \rangle} \right).
