Epistemic inquiry by bounded rational agents into empirical reality must assign probabilistic credences rather than absolute certainty (0 or 1), due to inductive underdetermination and finite sampling.

Review note
This is a standard position in Bayesian epistemology and the philosophy of induction: bounded agents cannot achieve absolute certainty about contingent empirical matters due to finite data and the problem of induction. The claim is correctly typed as conceptual, the refutation condition (a protocol for finite agents to attain deductive certainty about contingent empirical configurations) is properly demanding, and the evidence cites Cox's theorem and Jaynes. Confidence 0.95 is appropriate for a well-supported but still conceptual normative claim.

Evidence

Hume's problem of induction, Bayesian confirmation theory, and Cox's theorem establishing probability calculus as the unique coherent extension of classical logic under incomplete information.

Sources

  • Cox, R. T. (1946). Probability, Frequency and Reasonable Expectation. American Journal of Physics.
  • Jaynes, E. T. (2003). Probability Theory: The Logic of Science. Cambridge University Press.
